Morse

Morse is an encoding, not encryption. Anyone who hears, sees or reads the dots and dashes can decode them. Morse messages you send in a chat are protected by the normal end-to-end encryption, like any other message.

Translate

Type text to get Morse, or type/paste Morse to get text. Letters are separated by a space and words by /. Look-alike characters such as ·, • and — are accepted. Prosigns are written as <AR>, <SK> and so on. Anything that cannot be translated shows as �.

Optional shorthand turns common phrases into Q-codes and abbreviations (for example "best regards" → 73), and you can define up to 200 of your own abbreviations.

Reference chart

Letters, digits, punctuation and prosigns, with playback for each.

Tap keyer and manual entry

Tap or hold the key (mouse, touch or keyboard) to enter dots and dashes. The keyer shows each symbol as you go and decodes as you type, using your speed setting. You can also enter dots and dashes with buttons, which works well with screen readers.

Timing

Set the speed in words per minute (up to 40), Farnsworth spacing (extra space between characters to make learning easier) and the tone pitch.

Playback

  • Sound: a tone in your browser or app.
  • Light: the screen flashes. Speed is capped at 7 words per minute (at most three flashes per second), the flash is turned off when your system asks for reduced motion, and you are warned first because flashing light can affect people with photosensitive epilepsy.
  • Vibration: on Android through the app; in browsers only where the browser supports it (many desktop browsers do not). The app tells you when it is not available.
  • You can export a sequence as a sound file (.wav) or a text file (.txt), or copy it as text. Exported files are not encrypted.

Listen Experimental

Decodes Morse tones through your microphone. The microphone opens only when you press Start, after your browser or Android asks for permission, and audio never leaves the device. It works only with a single clean, steady tone between 300 and 1200 Hz, in a quiet room; speech, music, noise or two tones close together confuse it. It has not yet been tried with a real microphone on a phone.

Morse in chats

Insert Morse into a message from the chat composer or send from the Morse screen. Messages composed in Morse are marked with a Morse badge for the recipient. To make Morse that only your contact can read, use Secret Morse.

Secret Morse (automatic, with a contact)

Secret Morse turns your message into a code made from your and your contact's private keys. A new code is created for every message. Only your two phones can decode it — not relays, not Vexunori's makers, nobody else. People who hear or see the Morse only get the code. On your phone, decoded text stays hidden until you tap to reveal it (see Keeping Secret Morse private on your phone). It is modern encryption (very strong), not a one-time pad.

There are no pads to share: the code comes from the keys you already have for each other, so it works as soon as you are contacts.

  • In a one-to-one chat: tap the lock button next to the Morse button. While it is on, what you type is sent as a code, and drafts are not saved. Your contact sees “🔒 Secret Morse · Tap to reveal” and decodes it with their app passphrase or PIN (or automatically, if they turned Tap to reveal off). “Show code” shows the groups and their Morse, and Play opens them in the Morse player. The decoded text is only kept on screen; the saved message keeps the code. In group chats the button is hidden: Secret Morse works in one-to-one chats only.
  • By sound, light or radio: on the Morse screen, open Secret Morse, pick the contact, type the message and press Make code. Copy the groups or the Morse, or play them as sound, light or vibration. The other person picks you as the contact, pastes or taps in the groups (letters or Morse; spacing doesn't matter) and presses Decode.
  • What is kept: letters (accents removed), digits, spaces and . , ? ! ' - /. Other signs are left out and you are told which. Morse has no capitals, so messages arrive in capitals. Up to about 5,000 characters.
  • If decoding fails you see “Couldn't decode — not from this contact or damaged.” That means the wrong contact was picked, a group was copied wrongly, or someone changed it.
How it works (technical)

Both phones compute the same shared secret from your private key and the other person's public key (NIP-44 v2 conversation key: ECDH on secp256k1). Each message gets a fresh random 50-bit indicator, sent in the clear as the first two groups (letters and the digits 2–7). HKDF-SHA256 turns the shared secret and the indicator into two one-message keys. The text is mapped to the 36 symbols A–Z 0–9 (a space is 0, a digit d is 1d, . , ? ! ' - / are 2–8, and 9 fills the last group), and a ChaCha20 keystream, sampled without bias, is added mod 36. The last two groups are a check code (about 51 bits from HMAC-SHA256) that detects a wrong key or any change. Messages look like [2 indicator groups] [message groups] [2 check groups]; in a chat they start with VXSM1. With a secret phrase they start with VXSM2: the phrase is stretched with Argon2id (64 MiB, 3 passes; salt = SHA-256 of a fixed label and both public keys) and mixed into the HKDF input, so the keys need both the conversation key and the phrase. The phrase is never stored, and no check value is stored either, so nothing on the phone can be used to test guesses. Argon2id comes from hash-wasm; all other primitives come from the audited @noble libraries. The length of the message (in groups) is not hidden.

The manual one-time pad below is still available for when you want a key that does not depend on your phone's private key.

Keeping Secret Morse private on your phone

Secret Morse protects your messages on the way. These three options protect them on your phone, in case someone gets into the unlocked app. You can use all three.

1. Tap to reveal (on by default)

  • Secret Morse messages show only “🔒 Secret Morse · Tap to reveal”. To read one, tap it and enter the passphrase or PIN you use to unlock Vexunori. In a hidden profile, that profile's own passphrase or PIN.
  • After that, other Secret Morse messages in the same chat open without asking for 2 minutes.
  • The text hides again after 30 seconds (you can choose 10 seconds, 30 seconds or 2 minutes in Morse → Settings), when you leave the chat, when you switch to another app and when the app locks. The decoded text is never saved; only the code is.
  • Wrong tries get the same growing wait as the lock screen.
  • You can turn it off for all chats (Morse → Settings) or for one chat (the chat's Info page). Turning it off asks for your passphrase or PIN too.
  • No passphrase or PIN? (Android Keystore mode, or the web “no passphrase” mode.) Then revealing only asks you to confirm. That is only a privacy screen: anyone holding your unlocked phone can tap through it. Set a passphrase or PIN in Settings → Security for real protection.
  • Fingerprint (Android app): turn on Use fingerprint for Tap to reveal in Morse → Settings (it asks for your PIN or passphrase once). The reveal box then also offers Use fingerprint. Your fingerprint never leaves the phone and Vexunori never sees it; Android’s secure hardware checks it and only then lets Vexunori use a key that is locked to it, which must decrypt a small check value saved when you turned it on. Anyone whose fingerprint is set up on the phone can use it. The same waiting time after wrong tries and the same 2 minutes apply. Turning Tap to reveal off always needs your PIN or passphrase.

2. A secret phrase for one contact (strongest)

  • In a one-to-one chat, open Info and choose Add a secret phrase for Secret Morse. Agree on a phrase with your contact in person. It's never sent or saved. Without it, nobody can decode your Secret Morse — not even someone holding your unlocked phone. Your contact adds the same phrase in their app.
  • You type the phrase when you first reveal or send in that chat. The app keeps it in memory only until the app locks or has been in the background for 5 minutes, then wipes it.
  • The app keeps no copy and no check value, so it cannot tell you whether a phrase is right. A wrong phrase shows “Couldn't decode — wrong phrase or damaged”.
  • Use several words. A short phrase could be guessed by someone who has both your unlocked phone and the code, although each guess is made slow on purpose.
  • The Secret Morse tab on the Morse screen has a “Use a secret phrase” switch for codes you send by sound, light or radio.
  • Messages sent before you added a phrase still open without it.

3. Burn after reading

  • With Secret Morse on, the composer shows 🔥 Burn after reading. A chat can have it on by default (Info page).
  • Your contact can reveal the message once. As soon as it is shown, the saved copy is deleted on their phone; when the text hides they see “Burned after reading”. Their app then tells your apps to delete your copy too.
  • You can't reveal your own burn message after sending; you see “Sent — burns after reading”.
  • Deleted from both apps after one reading. A modified app, a screenshot (blocked on Android) or another camera can still keep a copy.

One-time pad (WW2-style secret Morse)

A one-time pad is unbreakable only if the pad is truly random, used once, shared securely, and destroyed after use. Lose or reuse the pad and the protection is gone. In a chat, messages are also end-to-end encrypted; the pad mostly matters when you send Morse by sound, light or radio, where anyone can listen.

In the Second World War, Morse itself was never secret. SOE agents, resistance groups and Soviet spies enciphered their text with a one-time pad first and keyed out only the cipher. Machine ciphers such as Enigma were broken; a correctly used one-time pad cannot be. The One-time pad tab on the Morse screen does the same thing.

  • Pads: 500 to 5,000 groups of 5 random letters on numbered pages of 50 groups, made with your device's secure random generator (without bias). Pads are kept in your encrypted vault. You can link a pad to a contact.
  • Who sends with what: by default you send with the first half of the pad and the other person with the second half, so you never both use the same letters. A "one-way" pad lets only the maker send.
  • Sharing a pad: show it as a series of QR codes for the other phone to scan, or print it as a paper pad. Share pads only face to face or over a channel you fully trust, never in the clear over the internet. You can also send a pad in a Vexunori chat, with a warning: the pad is then only as safe as that chat.
  • Enciphering: your text is turned into the letters A–Z (digits are spelled out, a full stop becomes X, spaces and other signs are left out). Each letter is added to the next pad letter mod 26 and the result is shown in 5-letter groups. The first group is a five-digit indicator, sent in the clear, that says where in the pad the message starts (for example 00051 = page 2, group 1). The used position is saved before the cipher is shown, and Vexunori refuses to encipher when your part of the pad is used up.
  • Sending: copy the groups, key them out as sound, light or vibration (Farnsworth spacing helps with groups), or insert them into a chat (in the chat composer: Insert Morse → "Encipher with one-time pad").
  • Deciphering: paste or tap in the groups (letters or Morse), pick the pad and press Decipher. You are warned loudly if those pad letters were already used to send a message from your device, which would mean a pad was used twice.
  • Destroying: "Destroy used pages" overwrites the used groups on your device, so old messages can no longer be deciphered there. "Delete pad" removes the pad. Paper copies and the other person's copy are not affected; destroy those yourself. Phones and computers may keep traces of overwritten data in storage for some time.

Limits: printing may not work inside the Android app, and pad transfer by QR code has not yet been tried on a real phone. Your security also depends on the other person keeping their copy safe. The Learn tab has a short history of one-time pads and Enigma.
